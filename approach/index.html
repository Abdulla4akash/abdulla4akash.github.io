Our Implementation Approach

Studying the conditions for responsible implementation in Bangladesh's health and public-service systems.

Promising technology is only one part of successful implementation. ISH studies the technical, clinical, economic, organisational, workforce, and policy conditions that determine whether a health intervention can work reliably in real-world settings.

Bangladesh's urban and rural health systems differ in workforce capacity, infrastructure, service workflows, access, resource availability, training requirements, and institutional constraints. Implementation therefore needs to be evaluated as a system, with attention to how an intervention would function responsibly within existing health and public services.

A pathway for inquiry

The sequence below organises our research questions. It is not a validated proprietary methodology or a claim that every ISH project has completed every stage. Studies may revisit earlier questions, investigate several stages together, or stop when the evidence does not support further implementation.

  1. Problem identification and context
  2. Technology and evidence assessment
  3. Clinical and public-health assessment
  4. Economic and financial feasibility
  5. Field and operational feasibility
  6. Workforce and training design
  7. Implementation and applied evaluation
  8. Policy, institutional adoption, and sustainability
  9. Decision: adapt, continue, stop, or scale cautiously

ISH's multidisciplinary structure provides complementary perspectives across these stages. The People profiles linked below describe relevant roles; they do not imply that every individual has worked on every project or that all stages are established operational capabilities.

1. Problem identification and context

Define the health-system problem before selecting a technology. A change to staffing, organisation, or an existing service may be more appropriate than a new technical intervention.

  • What service-delivery problem is being addressed, and who experiences it?
  • Which urban or rural settings are relevant?
  • What workflows, personnel, infrastructure, and constraints already exist?
  • Is a technological intervention appropriate, and what alternatives should be considered?

This stage establishes the overall research direction and implementation framing.

2. Technology and evidence assessment

Understand what a proposed technology can and cannot do. Digital tools, diagnostics, decision-support systems, and AI or machine learning are possible technologies to assess, according to the problem and available evidence.

  • What evidence supports the technology, and in which populations and settings was it generated?
  • How well might that evidence transfer to Bangladesh?
  • What technical resources and support are required?
  • What failure modes, uncertainty, and limitations are documented?

Relevant perspective: S M Abdulla Al Mamun — Principal Investigator.

3. Clinical and public-health assessment

Assess whether the intervention makes sense within health-service and population-health contexts. Technical performance alone does not establish clinical relevance or public-health value.

  • What clinical or public-health problem does it address?
  • How might it interact with healthcare workflows?
  • What barriers and facilitators could affect implementation?
  • Which service or health outcomes would be meaningful and appropriate to evaluate?

Relevant perspectives: Saddam Hossen — Research Associate — Public Health & Implementation Science; Dr Saheen Ahmed — Clinical Mentor. These roles contribute research and clinical perspectives; they do not confer clinical governance or regulatory authority.

4. Economic and financial feasibility

Investigate whether implementation is financially and economically plausible, including the conditions under which it could be sustained.

  • What resources and implementation and operating costs are likely to be required?
  • What affordability constraints exist?
  • What financing arrangements might be needed?
  • What trade-offs arise between alternative approaches?
  • Under what conditions would cost-effectiveness analysis be appropriate, and what evidence would it require?

Cost-effectiveness is an evaluation question, not an established result of this framework.

Relevant perspective: Tazwar Bin Saleh — Research Associate — Economics & Development Finance.

5. Field and operational feasibility

Study the practical conditions outside a controlled technical environment, including differences between urban and rural settings.

  • Can the intervention fit existing service workflows?
  • What infrastructure and implementation logistics would be required?
  • What operational bottlenecks may arise?
  • What barriers could affect users, workers, or communities, and how might these vary across settings?

These questions define field-feasibility research; they do not establish that field deployments have taken place.

Relevant perspective: Jannatul Ferdaus — Field Operations Associate.

6. Workforce and training design

Study the human capabilities required to implement and sustain an intervention. Training needs depend on staff roles, existing competencies, and the service environment.

  • Which staff would use or support the intervention, and what competencies would they need?
  • What training would be necessary, and how should competency be assessed?
  • How could implementation fidelity be maintained?
  • What happens when staff turnover or workload changes?

Training design is part of the research pathway; inclusion here does not imply completed training programmes.

Relevant perspective: Nabila Islam — Research Associate — Training & Workforce Development.

7. Implementation and applied evaluation

Evaluate what happens when an intervention is introduced into a real or realistically modelled system, where the study design and required approvals permit. Evidence from a model can inform further investigation; it cannot by itself establish performance in routine services.

Depending on the intervention, setting, and research question, evaluation may investigate:

  • adoption, reach, usability, and accessibility;
  • fidelity, workload, and effects on workflows;
  • operational reliability and resource use;
  • unintended consequences;
  • relevant service or health outcomes, where appropriate;
  • sustained performance over time.

The outcomes, measures, and limitations need to be specified for each study. Planning an evaluation is not evidence of successful implementation.

8. Policy, institutional adoption, and sustainability

Study what would be required for a promising intervention to become sustainably embedded in organisations or public services.

  • Which institutions would need to be involved?
  • What policy and governance conditions matter, and what evidence would decision-makers require?
  • What financing and organisational arrangements would be needed?
  • How could a pilot or study transition into sustained use?
  • What safeguards and further evaluation would be needed before wider adoption?

This stage concerns requirements for possible institutional use. It does not imply government influence, signed partnerships, or policy adoption.

Relevant perspective: Tanveer Mahmud — Policy & Partnerships Associate.

9. Decision: adapt, continue, stop, or scale cautiously

A technically successful intervention should not automatically progress toward wider implementation. Depending on the evidence, ISH may conclude that it should:

  • continue to the next evaluation stage;
  • be adapted and reassessed;
  • undergo further evidence generation;
  • remain limited to a specific setting;
  • or stop.

Wider implementation is an evidence-dependent decision. Cautious scale would require evidence relevant to the proposed settings, appropriate safeguards, and arrangements for continued evaluation and sustainability. It is not an assumed objective or a demonstrated outcome of the framework.

Connecting the approach to current research

Our Research in Progress page describes retrospective work on qPCR interpretation and resource-constrained malaria image analysis. These studies address bounded questions about evidence, expert review, and technical resources. They do not demonstrate completion of this implementation pathway, service adoption, or population-level impact.
